# Website Legal, Security & Accessibility Compliance — Master Prompt

**Use this on every website you (Joe / AI Growth Studio) build or maintain, for yourself or a client, before or shortly after launch.** Paste this whole file into a Claude Code session opened on the site's repo. It works standalone — if you don't already know the answers to the intake questions, it will ask you before drafting anything, so you never have to pre-fill it by hand.

---

## Step 0 — Intake (answer these before I draft anything, or tell me to proceed with placeholders)

1. Business/site name, and the legal entity behind it if different.
2. Business type: service, e-commerce, healthcare/regulated, rental/property, info/content site, other.
3. State(s) the business operates in or is licensed in (if a licensed profession — medical, legal, financial, contracting, etc.).
4. Does the site collect personal data through native forms (contact form, booking, signup, intake)? If yes, which forms and what fields.
5. Does the site handle payments directly, or link out to an external processor/booking system (Stripe, Jane, Calendly, etc.)?
6. Does the site publish customer testimonials or reviews?
7. Does the site make any specific outcome, results, ROI, or performance claims?
8. Any regulated content or industry angle: health, financial, legal, alcohol, firearms, children-directed, or similar?
9. What analytics/tracking is currently installed (Google Analytics, Meta Pixel, TikTok Pixel, Vercel Analytics, etc.)?
10. Is email or SMS marketing planned or already in use?
11. Where is the actual customer base located, versus the fact that the site is publicly reachable everywhere unless geo-blocked?

If I already know these answers (from project notes, a CLAUDE.md, or earlier in this conversation), state them back to me for confirmation instead of re-asking from scratch.

## Ground rules

I am not a lawyer, and neither is Joe. Draft every legal page and clause as a strong, standard first pass using established templates and general best practice — never invent business-specific facts (license numbers, refund terms, entity names, addresses). Use a clearly marked `[NEEDS OWNER INPUT]` placeholder instead of guessing. End with a flagged list of every clause that should get a quick read from a licensed attorney before publishing.

This is a compliance and risk-reduction pass, not a redesign — don't touch unrelated design/content unless a fix requires it.

## 1. Legal pages

Create as real routes, and link every one of them from the site's footer.

- `/privacy-policy` — what data is actually collected (per intake #4/#9), how it's used, any third parties/processors data flows through, cookie/tracking disclosure (see §2), how a visitor can exercise access/opt-out/delete rights, contact info, effective date.
- `/terms-and-conditions` — acceptable use, no-warranty / limitation-of-liability language, governing law (the business's home state), a dispute-resolution/arbitration clause marked `[NEEDS ATTORNEY REVIEW]` rather than drafted definitively.
- `/cookie-policy` — required if any non-essential tracking is present (see §2); otherwise a short essential-cookies-only notice folded into the privacy policy is enough.
- `/refund-policy` — only if the business takes payments or bookings (intake #5). Use `[NEEDS OWNER'S ACTUAL POLICY]` rather than inventing timelines, percentages, or conditions.
- If regulated content is involved (intake #8): add the matching disclaimer — medical-advice disclaimer, financial-advice disclaimer, legal-advice disclaimer, age-gate language, or an industry-specific licensure disclaimer stating which state(s)/jurisdictions the business is actually authorized to serve.
- `/accessibility` — a short, genuine WCAG 2.1 AA commitment statement with a real contact method for visitors to report barriers.

## 2. Cookies, analytics & third-party tracking

- Inventory every script actually loaded on the site (analytics, ad pixels, embeds, fonts, chat widgets, video hosts) and list all of them in your final summary.
- Any pixel or tag that ties an identified visitor to interest, health, or financial signals (Meta Pixel, TikTok Pixel, Google Ads conversion tag — especially alongside anything regulated per intake #8) is one of the most active current lawsuit categories in the US. Flag it before adding one; never add one by default just because it's easy analytics.
- If nothing does cross-site ad tracking, a clearly disclosed opt-out model is usually sufficient. A hard opt-in consent gate is only needed for non-essential cookies serving visitors in opt-in jurisdictions — flag that tradeoff for the owner to decide rather than assuming either way.

## 3. Accessibility (WCAG 2.1 AA) — fix the code, never bolt on a widget

Do not install an accessibility-overlay product (AccessiBe-style) as the fix. Recent lawsuit-trend data shows plaintiffs' firms specifically target overlay-only sites, and courts increasingly reject overlays as a defense. Fix the underlying markup instead:

- Contrast-check every text/background pairing against 4.5:1 (normal text) / 3:1 (large text, 18px+/14px-bold+, and UI elements like button borders) — fix the actual palette values, don't eyeball it.
- Every interactive element (buttons, links, nav, embedded booking/CTA links) must be reachable and operable by keyboard alone, with a visible focus ring — never strip `:focus` styles without replacing them.
- Every form input has a real, programmatically associated `<label>`, not just a placeholder attribute.
- Every meaningful image has real, descriptive alt text; purely decorative images get `alt=""`.
- Buttons and links use clear, specific text — no bare "click here" or icon-only controls without an `aria-label`.
- Add a skip-to-content link for keyboard users.
- Confirm the site is usable at 200% browser zoom without horizontal scrolling or clipped content.
- If the site links to any downloadable PDFs/forms, check whether they're tagged/accessible or flat scans — flag if the latter, since these are often the actual documents a visitor most needs.

## 4. Content integrity — where most FTC/consumer-protection risk actually lives

- Every testimonial/review must have documented, on-file authorization from the real person it came from — remove or flag anything invented, composited, or unauthorized.
- Flag and rewrite any unsubstantiated claim: "guaranteed," specific outcome numbers/timelines with no backing data, "clinically proven" without a citation, anything implying a certification or endorsement the business doesn't actually hold. Treat every outcome or results claim (intake #7) as guilty until it's backed by a real, citable source — this is one of the FTC's most actively enforced areas across nearly every industry, not just health.
- Verify every business detail displayed (name, address, phone, hours, license/registration numbers) matches reality — don't assume old placeholder content is still accurate.

## 5. Images, fonts & other third-party assets

- Audit every image, icon, and font used or referenced: confirm each is an original asset, a properly licensed stock asset (note the license type), or an open-license font (Google Fonts, etc.) — flag anything with no traceable license, especially anything that looks pulled from a generic image search.
- If any AI-generated image, video, voice, or written copy is used in a way a visitor could mistake for authentic or human-made (a "photo" of a real-looking person, an AI voice, an AI-written testimonial or bio), add a simple, visible disclosure. This is an active and growing legal requirement (FTC guidance plus a growing list of state laws), not just good practice — and note separately that AI-generated logos/images can carry murkier trademark or copyright registrability than human-made ones if the owner ever wants to register them.

## 6. Security hygiene

- Add standard security headers: Content-Security-Policy, X-Frame-Options, Strict-Transport-Security, Referrer-Policy, X-Content-Type-Options — via the framework's config (`next.config.js`, `vercel.json`, or equivalent).
- Run a dependency vulnerability audit (`npm audit` or the stack's equivalent) and report any high/critical findings.
- Confirm no API keys or secrets are exposed in the client-side bundle.
- If the site emails or notifies anyone on form submission, confirm the notification body never includes more personal or sensitive data than strictly necessary — especially anything regulated per intake #8.

## 7. Applicable law — quick reference

Federal baseline for any US business website: FTC Act (deceptive advertising/testimonials), ADA Title III (website accessibility — courts do not require the plaintiff or the business to share a state), CAN-SPAM (any email marketing), TCPA (any SMS marketing — requires explicit prior opt-in, not implied consent). Layer in state-specific requirements based on the business's actual state(s) of operation and where its customers really are (intake #3/#11) — note that New York and California generate a disproportionate share of ADA web-accessibility lawsuits nationally regardless of a business's physical location, since a public website itself is treated as the "place of public accommodation."

## 8. What to report back

End with a written summary covering: every page/file created or changed; every `[NEEDS OWNER INPUT]` or `[NEEDS ATTORNEY REVIEW]` placeholder left and exactly why; the full third-party script inventory from §2; the contrast-check results from §3; and the testimonial/claims audit results from §4. Never silently invent a business-specific fact anywhere in this process — placeholder it and flag it instead.
